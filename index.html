ACT 1

The single-digit illusion: unemployment fell because the labour market emptied out

The unemployment rate is a fraction with a moving denominator. When people stop looking for work they drop out of that denominator, and the rate falls without a single job being created. To measure how much of the improvement is real, we recompute the rate holding the participation rate constant .

DATA

Between 1395 and 1404 the population aged 15+ went from 59.5 to 66.1 million (+11.1%). Over the same period the employed rose by 2.30 million, the unemployed fell by 1.18 million, and the inactive population grew by 5.51 million.

Participation went from 43.2% to 40.6% and the employment ratio from 37.8% to 37.5% .

ANALYSIS

The adjusted rate shows two distinct phases. Between 1395 and 1398 the improvement was real : the adjusted rate also fell from 12.4% to 8.9% . From 1399 onward the two diverge; the official rate reaches 7.5% while the adjusted rate locks into a 12–14% band.

In other words, almost all of the reported improvement after 1399 is explained by the relative shrinking of the labour market, not by job creation.

CONCLUSION

The unemployment rate has become a misleading indicator for this economy. The alternative proposed here is the employment-to-population ratio , which fell 0.3 pp in nine years and tells the truer story: Iran's economy has not created jobs in proportion to its growing working-age population.

Any policy that measures its own success by the unemployment rate is exposed to this error.

ACT 2

The absent half: the country invests in women and then does not use them

For the first time in the available statistics, women form the majority of Iran's university students. In the very same year, women's labour force participation hit its lowest level of the decade. The two lines move in opposite directions.

DATA

Women's share of students rose from 46.1% in 1394–1395 to 51.9% in 1403–1404. Over the same period women's participation fell from 16.3% to 13.4% and their employment ratio from 12.9% to 11.4% .

Women are the majority in art (69.5%), basic sciences (68.7%), medicine (58.8%) and humanities (57.8%), and a minority in engineering (24.8%).

ANALYSIS

The fall in women's unemployment from 20.7% to 15.0% looks positive at first glance, but it happened alongside a fall in their employment ratio — the Act 1 pattern, only sharper. The official definition does not count an educated woman who has stopped actively searching as unemployed.

The mix of fields matters too: industry — which provides 32.8% of national employment — draws on a field where only a quarter of students are women.

CONCLUSION

Iran's largest untapped human resource is 28.7 million economically inactive women aged 15+ — a population larger than everyone currently employed in the country.

If women's participation returned merely to its 1397 level (17.6%), roughly 1.4 million people would join the labour force. That is the return on an investment the country has already made in educating them.

ACT 3

Warning bell, spring 1405: where the decade-long trend broke

The most recent quarterly data in this dataset marks the first serious reversal. Comparing spring 1405 with spring 1404 — like-for-like quarters, which cancels out seasonality — unemployment did not merely rise; the absolute number of employed people fell.

Why this quarter matters: Throughout 1395–1404, falling unemployment came alongside stable or slowly rising employment. Spring 1405 is the first quarter where both deteriorate at once: fewer jobs and more unemployed. This pattern cannot be explained by people leaving the labour market — the labour force stayed almost flat (+32 thousand). What changed is that 451 thousand people moved from the employed column to the unemployed one.

DATA

Spring 1405: population 15+ at 66,671 thousand (+828 thousand against spring 1404), labour force 27,136 (+32), employed 24,672 (−451), unemployed 2,464 (+483).

Industry's share of employment fell from 33.0% to 31.0% ; services rose from 52.4% to 53.8% and agriculture from 14.6% to 15.1% .

ANALYSIS

Translating shares into headcounts shows industry lost about 643 thousand jobs, while services added only 110 thousand and agriculture 58 thousand. The shock originates almost entirely in industry.

The simultaneous rise in agriculture's share — a sector shrinking throughout the decade — is the classic signature of labour taking refuge in informal, low-productivity work during a contraction.

CONCLUSION

This is a turning point, not noise. For the first time in the period studied, growth in the working-age population has coincided with an absolute decline in employment.

Methodological caveat: this finding rests on a single quarter and should be checked against summer and autumn 1405. That said, the size of the move (1.8 pp on the unemployment rate) exceeds any seasonal fluctuation recorded in this dataset.

ACT 4

The empty cradle: the demographic engine is shutting down

So far we have talked about the people who exist. This act is about those who do not. Registered births fell 41.6% in nine years — and the decline accelerated in the last two.

A dependable relationship: the correlation between marriages in a given year and births in the following year is 0.95 over this period (same-year correlation: 0.89). Because marriage is a time-lagged driver of births, the fall in marriages to 432 thousand in 1404 — the lowest of the period — means the decline in births continues at least through 1405 and is already locked into today's data.

DATA

Births: 1,528,053 in 1395 to 892,278 in 1404 (compound annual growth rate −5.8%). Deaths: 369,751 to 459,984 (+2.5% per year). Natural increase: 1,158,302 down to 432,294.

Winter 1404, with 207,578 births, is the lowest-birth quarter recorded in this dataset.

ANALYSIS

Two forces are at work at once: falling births, rooted in fewer marriages and smaller households, and rising deaths, which are largely a product of population ageing rather than deteriorating health. 1399 and 1400 are the COVID exception; after that, deaths returned to their long-run trend.

The fall in rural births (−43.5%) is slightly steeper than in cities (−41.0%), meaning the rural fertility advantage is also disappearing.

CONCLUSION

Iran's natural population increase has shrunk by nearly two-thirds in nine years. This is no longer a slow trend; it accelerated in 1403 and 1404.

Because marriage leads births and marriages hit a period low in 1404, a continued near-term decline is close to certain. Pro-natalist policies, if they are having an effect, are not yet visible in the available data.

ACT 5

The school queue is getting shorter: a wave already on its way

School enrolment peaked at 16.97 million in 1401–1402, an echo of the 1393–1396 birth wave. That wave is over, and what follows is far smaller — something we know not from any forecast but from the number of children already born .

Total births from 1395 to 1400 come to 7,808,954; from 1399 to 1404, 6,235,729.

ANALYSIS

School enrolment mirrors the birth wave with a six-to-seven-year lag. Since births have fallen continuously from 1395, enrolment enters a sustained decline from around 1405–1406: first in primary, then with a lag in secondary and higher education.

The 43% drop in graduates happened while enrolment has been rising since 1400 — pointing to a mix of weak intakes in the intervening years and, plausibly, more dropouts or longer time-to-degree.

CONCLUSION

The country is on the verge of a major reorganisation of educational infrastructure: surplus capacity in primary schools within a few years, then in secondary schools and universities.

On the other side, the flow of young workers into the economy will be markedly thinner after about 1415. Iran's demographic window — the period when the working-age share is high — is closing, while (per Act 1) it has yet to be fully used.

ACT 6

Official projections under strain

The "Population and Human Resources" sheet places two things side by side that are rarely compared: the population projection to 1415 and actual birth and death statistics. Put them together and a gap appears — one that has widened rapidly in the last two years.

DATA

The official projection implies a population increase of 601 thousand for 1404, while actual natural increase that year was 432 thousand — a gap of 169 thousand. In 1403 the gap was 111 thousand.

The projected annual population growth rate falls from 1.12% in 1397 to 0.36% in 1415. The rural population goes from 20.75 to 18.20 million (−12.3%).

ANALYSIS

The gap has two distinct origins and should not be read as one. The 1399 and 1400 gaps (+97 and +74 thousand) come from excess COVID deaths, an event no projection anticipated. The 1403 and 1404 gaps (+111 and +169 thousand) have a different source: births falling faster than the model assumed.

The scenarios show that if the current trend continues, natural increase reaches zero somewhere between 1412 (trend continuation) and 1415 (moderated scenario), and turns negative thereafter.

CONCLUSION

This is not a criticism of the Statistical Center's model; every demographic projection rests on assumptions and must be revised when reality moves. Our finding is that that moment has arrived: the gap has doubled in two years, and the cause is identifiable.

Practical implication: planning that relies on 91.4 million people in 1415 — from subsidy and water allocation to school capacity and pension funds — should also accommodate a lower scenario of roughly 88 to 89 million.

Conclusions

Two hourglasses emptying at once

This dashboard's argument is one sentence: Iran faces two constraints at the same time, and each makes the other worse. On one side the demographic engine is shutting down, and the inflow of workers will be smaller in the decades ahead. On the other, right now — while the working-age population is still growing — the economy has failed to absorb it, and the official unemployment rate has concealed that failure. The demographic window is closing before it was ever really opened.

1 · Change the indicator

In an economy with falling participation, the unemployment rate is a misleading measure. employment-to-population ratio and the absolute number of employed people should be the primary yardstick for employment policy. The former fell 0.3 pp in nine years.

2 · The largest reserve is women

28.7 million women aged 15+ are outside the labour force, even though women are the majority of university students. Returning women's participation merely to its 1397 level would add around 1.4 million people to the labour force.

3 · Take spring 1405 seriously

The first quarter in which jobs fell and unemployment rose together, with a clear origin in industry (−643 thousand jobs, estimated). It needs checking against the next two quarters, but its magnitude is unprecedented.

4 · Revise the projections

The gap between the official projection and actual natural increase has more than doubled in two years. Long-term planning should cover a scenario of 88 to 89 million in 1415, not only 91.4 million.

5 · The education clock rings first

The decline in school enrolment begins around 1405–1406 and, unlike the labour market, is close to certain — the children are already born. Surplus school capacity is an opportunity for reallocation, not a crisis.

6 · Where the two crises meet

Unless productivity and participation rise, a shrinking future workforce means lower output and compounding pressure on pension funds. The time to use the young population that already exists is now.

3 · Derived indicators and how they are computed

a) Participation-adjusted unemployment — this project's central indicator

The question: if the participation rate had stayed at its base-year level (base), with the same actual number of employed people, what would the unemployment rate be?

A*t = Pt × rbase   ← counterfactual labour force
U*t = ( A*t − Et ) / A*t = 1 − Et / ( Pt × rbase )
Ht = A*t − At   ← hidden unemployed

where P is the population aged 15+, E the employed population, A the published labour force and r the participation rate. Worked example for 1404 with a 1395 base (43.2%): A* = 66,148 × 0.432 = 28,576 thousand; U* = 1 − 24,822 / 28,576 = 13.14%; hidden unemployed = 28,576 − 26,837 = 1,739 thousand.

Limitation: this indicator does not assume everyone who left is truly unemployed. Part of the fall in participation may reflect more education, retirement, or an ageing population structure. U* should therefore be read as an upper bound on true unemployment and U as a lower bound. The analytical message is the distance between them.

b) Gender breakdown of the 15+ population and employment (analytical estimate)

The file does not publish the 15+ population by gender, but it does give men's and women's employment ratios. The breakdown can be reconstructed by solving a single-unknown equation:

rm·Pm + rf·(P − Pm) = E   ⟹   Pm = ( E − rf·P ) / ( rm − rf )

For 1404 with P = 66,148, E = 24,822, rm = 63.8%, rf = 11.4%: 32,979 thousand men and 33,169 thousand women; 21,041 thousand employed men and 3,781 thousand employed women ⟹ women's share of employment 15.2%.

Independent validation: the breakdown was reconstructed using the gender-specific participation rates and Unemployment rate — which were not used in the calculation: the reconstructed labour force comes to 26,837.5 against a published 26,837 (error 0.002%) and reconstructed unemployment to 2,010 against 2,014 (error 0.19%). This agreement indicates the estimated breakdown is reliable.

c) Sector employment and the spring 1405 shock
Es,t = shares,t × Et

Spring 1404: 33.0% × 25,123 = 8,291; spring 1405: 31.0% × 24,672 = 7,648 ⟹ change −643 thousand.

Rounding error: shares are published to one decimal place (±0.05 ), equivalent to roughly ±12 thousand people. The change observed in industry is 50 times that error, but smaller changes (agriculture +58 thousand) should be read cautiously.

d) Potential primary-school entry cohort
Ct = Σi=0..5 Bt+i   ← sum of six consecutive years of births

Comparing C(1395) = 7,808,954 with C(1399) = 6,235,729 ⟹ −20.1% across a four-year shift of the window.

Assumptions: near-universal enrolment, negligible child mortality, zero net migration. This calculation does not forecast the level of future enrolment — only the relative shrinkage of cohorts, which is highly insensitive to those assumptions.

e) Population scenarios and the birth–death crossover
Bt+1 = Bt·(1+gb)  ·  Dt+1 = Dt·(1+gd)  ·  Pt+1 = Pt + Bt+1 − Dt+1
t* = ln(B0/D0) / ln( (1+gd) / (1+gb) )   ← crossover year

gb = −5.80% is the actual compound growth rate of births over 1395–1404, and gd = +2.46% the equivalent for deaths; the death figure is confirmed by computing the post-COVID period (1398–1404: 2.56%). The moderated scenario halves the fall in births (−3.5%) to allow for the possible effect of support policies and a floor on fertility. Crossover: 1412 (trend continuation) to 1415 (moderated). Net migration is assumed zero in both scenarios.

4 · What we do not claim

  • We do not claim the official statistics are wrong. The Statistical Center's definition of unemployment is the ILO international standard. Our critique concerns the adequacy of that indicator for this economy, not its correctness.
  • We do not claim all 5.5 million who stayed inactive want work. Some are certainly students, elderly or retired.
  • We do not claim the Statistical Center's projection is wrong. We say its assumptions have drifted from the last two years of data and warrant revision.
  • The extrapolated scenarios are not forecasts; they are sensitivity tests and are highly sensitive to the assumption that the trend continues.
  • The spring 1405 analysis rests on a single quarter. It is a solid finding but needs confirmation from subsequent quarters.
  • No causal relationship is claimed; the marriage–birth correlation is a time-lagged association, not proof of causation.

5 · Tools and workflow

  • Extraction and testing: a Python script using openpyxl read the sheet, and an automated consistency test was run over every table with a "total = components" structure. The nine inconsistencies reported in section 2 are the output of that test.
  • Calculations: every derived indicator was computed in code and checked by cross-validation (section 3b). No figure is quoted from memory; all are computed from the file itself.
  • Implementation: the charts are drawn as raw SVG with no external library, so the file works fully offline.

Responsibility for the accuracy of the data, calculations and inferences rests with the author, Milad Shabani. That is why every key figure in section 3 is shown with its formula and inputs, so it can be audited.
